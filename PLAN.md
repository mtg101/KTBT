# KTBT: Plan

What we're going to build next for [KTBT](CONCEPT.md). Stages are listed in the order we'll build them, and the first one is up next. Each stage ends with something you can run and watch.

When a stage is finished, it moves out of this file and into [DEVLOG.md](DEVLOG.md). AI quirks and how we handle them are in [LLM-TUNING.md](LLM-TUNING.md).

Every list item follows the same pattern: a **bold label**, a colon, then the details.

## Stages

- **Each bar's own menu (done 16 Nov):** every bar gets its own menu: real pocha food chosen to suit the owner, one or two signature dishes from their story, specials by weather, season and the owner's whim, and a menu board you can see in the tent. Built in fenced pieces, so it adds almost nothing to `visit.gd` (see **Each bar's own menu: the plan**, below). Taken before the refactor on purpose: most of it is rules, content and save data, away from the night code.
- **To watch, from the first nights with menus (16 Nov):** new regulars come quickly (10 after three nights at a new bar: the chance of becoming one may need lowering); rice ordered as a meal (a prompt nudge for now, a rule if it stays); table games rarely taken up (only the apartment game so far); big moments mostly sad; recaps' cliffhangers the night can answer. Each is in the stats, so the next few nights' logs will say.
- **The refactor, from step 4 (now, promised):** splitting `visit.gd` into parts with one job each, behaviour unchanged (the plan is under **A proper refactor**, in Essentials below). Straight after the menus, and nothing else goes before it: bad visits, customers coming in together and the second voice all live in the night code, so they wait for it. It also takes over the menus' one stopgap (see below).
- **Real art, sound and polish:** today's art, sound and music are placeholders made in code (the tent, the people, the weather, the ambience and Arirang). Replace them with real assets (see **Character art** and **Ambience** in the later ideas), finish the background animation, and style the UI. Add visual (screenshot) tests once the art settles.
- **The game:** the player joins the conversations, probably as the owner.

## Each bar's own menu: the plan

What it's for: today every bar has the same thirteen items, so every bar's nights sound alike. A bar's menu says who its owner is (an ex-chef's fancier plates, a fisherman's widow's seafood), gives customers something to talk about, and gives the owner's story somewhere to surface. The menu stays a game fact (it drives orders, the checks, the bill, the drawings and the hovers): the game owns what's possible, the AI chooses and flavours it within limits.

- **The fences (so it doesn't add to the debt):** no menu logic goes in `visit.gd`, only calls to the pieces below (expected: well under 100 lines there). Today's menu becomes the **classic menu**: old bars keep it, and so do the tests, so the 483 run unchanged. Each step below ends with something you can run and watch.
- **Step 1, the menu as data (done 16 Nov):** `Menu` reads the open bar's menu instead of a fixed list (`Menu.use(bar)` when a bar opens; the classic menu when there's none), and everything that asks it (orders, the bill, the prompts, the glossary, the dish drawings, the canned AI) gets that bar's. Saved with the bar: the ids of catalogue dishes, and signature dishes in full. The glossary's and Hangul tables forget the old menu when the bar changes. **The stopgap:** "the open bar's menu" is a setting on `Menu` until the refactor's `Night` object owns it.
- **Step 2, the catalogue (done 16 Nov: 50 dishes so far):** `rules/catalogue.gd`, 60 to 100 real pocha dishes and drinks, each with its Hangul, name, description, category, portion, price range, cooking time, other names (for the order checks), its drawing (`art`, from `rules/dish_art.gd`, with new vessels as they're needed: a stone pot, a skillet), and tags (spicy, seafood, comfort, fancy, cheap; summer or winter). By category: drinks (soju, beer, makgeolli, somaek, fruit wines), stews and soups (eomuk-tang, kimchi jjigae, budae jjigae, haemul-tang), pancakes (kimchi-jeon, haemul-pajeon, bindaetteok), noodles (janchi guksu, bibim guksu, naengmyeon in summer), seafood (golbaengi muchim, grilled clams), the grill and fryer (dak-ttongjip, dak-gangjeong, twigim), rice (jumeokbap, bokkeumbap) and sides (gyeran-mari, dubu kimchi, sundae, tteokbokki). The "not served here" check becomes the catalogue minus the bar's menu, so another bar's dish is never served here.
- **Step 3, a bar's menu, chosen when it opens (done 16 Nov):** a pocha street is recognisable from tent to tent, with each one's character in the rest, so a menu comes in layers (each catalogue dish is marked **always**, **classic** or **own**):
  - **Always, at every bar:** soju, beer and a bowl of rice (gonggibap).
  - **The pocha classics, most of them:** at least four of tteokbokki, odeng (or eomuk-tang), sundae, a pancake (pajeon, kimchi-jeon or haemul-pajeon), gyeran-mari and a late-night noodle (ramyeon or udon); makgeolli most of the time.
  - **Its own picks:** three to five more from the catalogue that suit the owner (from their archetype and story: seafood for a fisherman's widow, the grill for an old soldier, fancier plates for an ex-chef), including at least one hearty main (a stew, the grill or seafood).
  - **Its signature dishes:** one or two (step 4).
  - **How it's chosen: the AI chooses, the game keeps the house rules:** 12 to 16 items in all, about two-thirds a pocha anyone would know. The game adds the always dishes itself; the AI sees the owner and the whole catalogue (every dish's description, tags and price range) and chooses which classics this tent keeps, its own dishes, and each price within its range, with a line on why (kept with the bar, for the diary and the board later). The checks enforce the counts, the prices, a hearty main and that everything's in the catalogue; a reply that breaks a rule goes back with the reason, as orders do. Only if the AI keeps failing (or the game's offline) does the game build a menu by the same rules itself. The counts are easy to tune once we've seen a few bars.
- **Step 4, signature dishes (done 16 Nov):** the AI invents one or two dishes from the owner's story ("her mother's kimchi jjigae, the recipe her son loved"), giving the name, its Hangul, a one-line meaning (checked like names: the Hangul against the romanisation; no brands, no made-up Korean, nothing off the catalogue's categories), a description, and its drawing from the game's lists (vessel, bits, garnish, a colour). The game sets the price and cooking time (the AI picks the category from the catalogue's). The meaning is kept, so the hover never needs translating live. Built: `rules/signatures.gd`; a bar opened before them gets theirs once; the game has four of its own for when the AI can't. Later: the owner's signature as tonight's special (step 5), and the diary mentioning it.
- **Step 5, specials (done 16 Nov):** tonight's specials come from the bar's own menu, by the weather (as now) and the season (cold noodles in summer, stews in winter: the catalogue's tags), the owner's signature dish as "tonight's special" now and then, and now and then something sold out (a game fact: the board crosses it out, and the order prompt knows). Built: `rules/specials.gd`; never a staple (soju, beer, rice, somaek, soda), never last night's again (except the rain's pancake and makgeolli); sold out can't be ordered. Later: the specials chalked on the board itself, and a regular disappointed their usual's run out.
- **Step 6, a menu board you can see (done 16 Nov: sold out waits for step 5):** one board in each tent, in the bar's style (wall, floor A-frame, paper on the pole, lightbox); click it for the whole menu, specials first, the favourite marked 인기, sold out struck through. Later: the board's style chosen with the bar's look (by the AI, from the game's options), and the specials chalked on it.
- **Step 7, menus that change slowly (done 16 Nov):** every few weeks of nights, a dish nobody orders may go and a new one come in from the catalogue (never a regular's usual while they're still coming), and the owner's diary mentions it. Later: dishes the regulars ask for. Built: `rules/menu_changes.gd`: from three weeks of nights, now and then, the bar's own pick ordered least (at most twice in its last 60 visits, and nobody's usual) comes off, and the AI chooses what goes on from four seasonal dishes the game offers; told in the transcript, in the highlights, the stats and the diary. Not yet: dishes the regulars ask for.
- **Tests, as we go:** switching bars switches menus (and the glossary forgets the old one); old bars get the classic menu; the catalogue complete (every dish's Hangul has a meaning, every drawing fits its box, other names unique); a bar's menu keeps the rules; signature dishes checked; specials only from the bar's menu; the board matches what can be ordered.
- **Later, not in this stage:** dishes drawn by a local image model (see **AI-generated food art**), and players editing the catalogue (see **Player customisation**).

## Later ideas (not committed)

Grouped by type. Within each group, roughly in order of importance.

### Essentials (needed before anyone else plays it)

- **Content boundaries (a setting):** the AI writes about late-night drinking and K-drama heartbreak, so it will sometimes drift somewhere dark or crude. A **content setting** chooses how far it may go, e.g. **Cosy** (gentle, no swearing), **Drama** (the default: PG-13 heartbreak, mild language) and **Mature** (stronger language and heavier themes, handled with care). The setting goes into the style guide for every prompt, and the game also checks lines against it and rejects ones that cross it, the same way it rejects wrong titles. Some limits apply at every setting: nothing sexual beyond a kiss implied off-screen, no hateful slurs, no real-world politics, and themes like self-harm are never described or encouraged: if they come up, the owner responds with care. Matters more now customers chat to each other late at night (flirting and dark turns), and most of all once players can type to customers.
- **Speed and waiting:** each visit is 10+ AI calls, so prepare ahead instead of waiting. Partly done: the AI writes the next line while you're reading the last, the owner's hands carry food over while the AI thinks, greetings are quicker when it's busy, a new customer comes in two quick steps (name, then details), and the next new customer is invented ahead while the owner's free, so only their order waits when they arrive. Still to do:
  - **Warm the model up at the title screen:** Ollama unloads the model after it's been idle (`keep_alive`, 30 minutes), and the first reply after that can take up to a minute while it loads back into the GPU: on the loading screen, it looks stuck. The title screen could send a tiny request as it opens, so the model's ready by the time a bar's picked.
  - **A second voice:** the owner's AI voice is busy about 98% of the night (first drinks took 11–19 minutes on average, and customer chats got 8–21% of lines), because one AI request runs at a time. Customer chats could run on a second voice alongside the owner's conversations: two requests at once. Ollama can serve two together (`OLLAMA_NUM_PARALLEL`), but on one GPU each is slower, so measure it on the desktop and the laptop before relying on it; the game would need two AI objects, and chats that never wait for the owner's.
- **Settings and accessibility:** partly done: an **Options** panel (the ⚙ button, or Esc) holds reading pauses, stats, bubbles, captions, sound and music. Still to add, there and on the title screen: text size, reading pauses (a checkbox for now, off; perhaps a reading speed instead), how fast time runs when the tent's quiet (`TIME_SPEEDS` in `rules/night_rules.gd`), how busy the tent gets (about twenty customers a night now; a real pojangmacha might get thirty), volumes (sound and music, each with a tick box and slider, are in the Options panel; voices to come), content setting, AI backend and model (today these live in each machine's `ai_settings.cfg`), voice mode, full-screen, and the debug stats panel (F3; shown by default while we're testing). Plus accessibility options that cost little in a text-heavy game: bigger text, high contrast, and pausing between lines until the player clicks.
- **Older saves kept up to date:** for now, while we're building, old saves can fall behind: a bar saved before a feature existed mostly goes without it (bars from before the cat have no cat, and bars from before looks get the plain default look). A few things are already filled in when an older bar opens: an owner from before people had looks gets one from their age, and the owner and cat get their names in Hangul. Before anyone else plays, saves need upgrading as the game changes (each save already has a `version`): fill in anything new when an older bar is opened, such as giving it a cat, without asking the AI.
- **Packaging, first-run setup and releasing:** a big part of the point is learning how to ship a Godot game.
  - **Exporting:** Godot's export templates, a Windows export preset, an icon, the game's name and version in the build, and checking the exported build runs on a clean machine (no editor, no project folder: saves and logs go to the user folder).
  - **First run:** check for Ollama and the model, offer offline mode if they're missing, and point to setup help. Without this, nobody but the developer can run it.
  - **Releasing:** versions (the save's `version` too, so older saves can be upgraded: see **Older saves kept up to date**), release notes from the DEVLOG, licences for everything bundled (`LICENSE-ASSETS.md`), and an itch.io page (perhaps with butler for uploads). Maybe automated builds on GitHub later.
  - **A store page, as if for real:** everything an itch.io or Steam page needs, made the way we'd make it for a real release:
    - **The overview:** a one-line pitch ("Coffee Talk with soju: a Korean tent bar where every customer is invented by an AI, and every night is an episode"), a short description, a longer "about this game", features as bullets (AI customers with memories, regulars who come back, Korean TV captions, the owner's diary, Hangul names, offline mode), and tags.
    - **Screenshots:** five or six moments that sell it, taken in the game at full resolution: a full house in the rain, a TV caption mid-pop, a reaction ("충격!"), the 3am diary page, the "Previously on…" card, the title screen. Perhaps a way to take them from the game (a key, or a screenshot mode with the panels hidden).
    - **A promo video:** a 30–60 second trailer cut like a K-drama teaser: the tarp going up, customers ducking in, captions popping, a big moment, the diary, ending on the title card. Recorded from the game, with Arirang (or another of our public-domain arrangements) under it, and the game's own stings.
    - **The rest:** a cover image and a banner (from the title and loading screens' art), a short GIF or two, system requirements (an NVIDIA GPU with about 10GB for the local model, or offline mode without one), what the AI does and doesn't do (it runs on your machine; nothing's sent anywhere), credits and licences, and a press kit page.
    - **Being open about the AI:** the page says plainly that the characters are written by a local AI model and the game was built with AI help, as the project is.

- **A proper refactor (steps 0 to 3 of 7 done: see the DEVLOG):** the code's grown a stage at a time, and `visit.gd` is about 4,500 lines doing everything at the tent: drawing it, the people, conversations, chats, the cat, captions and reactions, regulars, the night and the diary. Split it into parts with one job each (the tent's drawing, people and their looks, conversations, customer chats, the cat, TV captions, regulars, the night and its diary), each with its own tests, as `rules.gd` was (now `scripts/rules/`, one file per area). Still to do: step 4, split `visit.gd`; step 5, a safe way for code that waits on the AI to notice the bar closed; step 6, upgrading older saves in one place, and a cap on how many diary entries a bar keeps; step 7, folders and ARCHITECTURE.md to match. Keep every test passing at every step, and change no behaviour while doing it. Next after the menus (see Stages). One thing it takes over from them: the open bar's menu, a setting on `Menu` until then, belongs to the `Night` object.
  - **Step 4, splitting `visit.gd` (started 16 Nov).** Decided: a `Night` object for what the parts share (it comes in with the first part that changes that state: the drawing parts only read it, so they're given what they need), and a part or two at a time, each committed with the tests green. Done: the pre-check (as `tests/test_code.gd`, run with the tests), part 1, the tent's look (`scripts/bar/tent_view.gd`), part 2, drawing people (`scripts/bar/people_view.gd`), and part 3, the street (`scripts/bar/street.gd`), which brought in the `Night` object (`scripts/bar/night.gd`: so far tonight's weather and stats; `visit.gd` reads and writes them through it, so there's one copy). Part 4, the cat (`scripts/bar/cat.gd`): where it is and drawing it; when it moves, and what happens because of it (moments, thefts), stay with the night until the conversations move. Part 5, the stove (`scripts/bar/kitchen.gd`): the queue and how dishes look; serving and carrying over stay with the night (they're the owner's hands and voice). Part 6, TV captions on screen (`scripts/bar/captions_on_screen.gd`): pacing, popping, the red light; what deserves one (events, the AI's reaction check and its queue) stays with the night. Part 7, the night as a story (`scripts/bar/story_keeper.gd`): the transcript file and what "Previously on…" is written from; writing the diary and playing the recap (the AI, the screens) stay with the night for now. Part 8, regulars tonight (`scripts/bar/regulars_tonight.gd`): who's coming in, their facts, how they're announced, drifting away, what the owner remembers; asking the AI (founding regulars, tonight's mood, memories after a visit) stays with the night, and so does friendliness (who calls whom), which goes with the conversations. Part 9, customer chats (`scripts/bar/counter_chats.gd`): who's chatting with whom and choosing the next spell; saying each line (the AI, the moments, the rituals) stays with the night, with the owner's conversations. Next: part 10, conversations with the owner, then the screen around it.
  - **The plan, as thought over before starting:** It's 4,329 lines: 207 functions and 122 variables, in 17 sections, from the owner and the night to drawing people and who calls whom. Everything big on the list goes through it (bad visits, per-bar menus and the menu board, a second AI voice, the game stage), so it's split first.
    - **Before anything moves, a pre-check:** a small script run on every change, before the tests: calls with too few arguments (a test that wouldn't load, 15 Nov), locals that clash with gdUnit's or Godot's own names (`before`, `any_object`, `ready`), and spaces where tabs should be. It catches in a second what otherwise only shows when Godot loads the file.
    - **How the parts share what's going on (the main decision):** most of `visit.gd` reads and changes the same few things: who's at the counter, the clock, the owner, the cat, tonight's events. Three ways to share them:
      - **A night object (suggested):** a plain `Night` holding that state (guests, clock, owner, weather, specials, the events), which each part is given. Parts change it and announce what happened through `night_events.gd`, which already feeds the stats, captions and diary. Easy to give a part a made-up `Night` in a test.
      - **Parts get the bar itself:** simplest to do, but every part can still reach everything, so it's tidier rather than better.
      - **Only signals:** the most separate, but hard to follow (what answered that?) and a lot of wiring.
    - **The parts, in the order they'd move** (least risk first: drawing only reads, so it goes first; the conversations, which everything touches, go last):
      1. **The tent's look** (`bar/tent_view.gd`, about 300 lines): the tarp, sign, sky and light, weather, bulbs, steam.
      2. **Drawing people** (`bar/people_view.gd`, about 350): looks, both views, umbrellas, phones, running for it, the owner's apron.
      3. **The street** (`bar/street.gd`, about 150): strollers, groups, passers-by who look in, rain gear on the way in and out.
      4. **The cat** (`bar/cat.gd`, about 150): where it goes, drawing it, its moments, what it's doing for prompts and checks.
      5. **The stove and serving** (`bar/kitchen.gd`, about 200): the queue, cooking, carrying dishes over, the banchan, the bill.
      6. **TV captions and reactions** (`bar/captions_on_screen.gd`, about 250): popping and pacing captions, the reaction check and its queue, the red light.
      7. **The night as a story** (`bar/story_keeper.gd`, about 300): the transcript file, the diary at 3am, "Previously on…", the summary panel.
      8. **Regulars and friendliness** (`bar/regulars_tonight.gd`, about 450): who comes back tonight, memories after a visit, becoming regulars, "can I call you eonni?".
      9. **Customer chats** (`bar/counter_chats.gd`, about 300): starting, joining, the owner joining in, sharing moments.
      10. **Conversations with the owner** (`bar/conversation.gd`, about 400): lines, situations, address notes, later orders, goodbyes.
      11. **The screen around it** (`bar/top_bar.gd`, `bar/options_panel.gd`, about 400): the top bar, the Options panel, the info and stats panels.
    - **What `visit.gd` keeps (aim: under 800 lines):** the night's clock and its loop, arrivals and the queue, what the owner does next (greet, settle up, a put-off reaction, invent ahead, chat, talk), and putting the parts together.
    - **Tests as it goes:** the click-through tests stay as they are, as the safety net, and must pass after every part. Tests that call the bar's private functions (`scene._move_cat`, `scene._react`) are pointed at the part instead. Each part that can be made on its own (with a made-up `Night`) gets tests of its own, so most new tests stop needing a whole night to run.
    - **One part per commit,** each with "behaviour unchanged" and the test count, so any problem points at one move. Folders (`scripts/bar/`) come with the parts, rather than as step 7 at the end.
    - **To decide before starting:** the night object or not (above); whether parts are Nodes in the scene (drawing ones, which have children) or plain objects (the logic ones); and whether this happens in one long session or a part or two at a time between other work.

### Richer play

- **Follow-ups from the Night as a story stage:**
  - **TV reactions:** at a 4 or 5 out of 5, only 7 showed in six nights (15 Nov), so the bar is now 3, and a check put off while the owner was busy waits for her instead of being lost. Watch from the stats (asked, shown, none, put off) whether the 3s feel like real moments; back to 4 if they feel cheap.
  - **The diary and the recap:** watch real entries and recaps for repeated openings, the same few moments every night, or facts bent out of shape; more stamps and recap styles as ideas come (a stormy-night horror-drama style is in; a sports-broadcast one for a record night).
  - **More from the story:** the diary book could show a night's TV reactions and line of the night beside the entry; "Previously on…" could show the people it's about, drawn small in their looks; a season recap after every ten episodes.
- **Follow-ups from the 15 Nov playtest review** (six nights, about 115 customers):
  - **Hints the AI picks up:** the served and sharing hints (pouring for each other, ssam, the pot lid) are counted with whether the line seemed to take them up. In the first nights, somaek's was taken up every time and the rest hardly at all. Give it a few more nights of stats, then reword or drop the ones that never land.
  - **Samey openings:** orders and the owner's first words now get situations now and then; the stats show the commonest start ("ajumma i'll" was 67 of 115 orders). Check it's come down.
  - **What gets ordered:** tteokbokki was about 60% of orders, and dakgalbi none for two nights. The menu's shuffled for each order now, with weather specials and cravings; check the spread in the stats' Orders line.
  - **Every visit goes well:** 1 visit in 115 was "didn't enjoy". A rough night now and then (a long wait, a dish that took ages, someone rude at the next stool) would give the diary and regulars more to work with.
  - **Regulars coming back:** only the two founding regulars came back; new regulars' pull averaged about 0.5. Look at whether they come back often enough to feel like regulars.
  - **The owner's one voice:** busy 98% of the night; first drinks took 11–19 minutes on average and customer chats were 8–21% of lines. See **Speed and waiting**.
- **Follow-ups from the Regulars and relationships stage:**
  - **Contradictions between nights:** a regular who was widowed one night had "my wife's nagging" the next. Check the things that stick catch family facts said in chats, and that prompts lean on them. (The owner's side is fixed: the owner's family and past are game facts now.)
  - **Terms of address in every line:** watch whether "most lines need none" brought it down.
  - **Dropping polite speech (말 놓을까요? mal noeulkkayo?, "shall we speak casually?"):** the K-drama beat where two people agree to drop jondaetmal (polite speech) for banmal (casual). It belongs with the friendlier-term moment we already have: when the younger asks to use a friendlier term, they could also ask to speak casually, with its own caption, and the game would remember it (the door already greets an old friend casually, 왔어?). Later, a stranger slipping into banmal too soon could be a small rudeness the other notices.
  - **What carries over from night to night (check after the menus):** list exactly what each regular brings to their next visit (their memories of the owner and the owner's of them, the things that stick, closeness, names known, their usual), what passes between regulars who chat, and what of last night (the diary, "Previously on…", tonight's facts) reaches tonight's prompts, and look for gaps: a night that should be remembered and isn't, or something carried that goes stale.
  - **Regulars knowing each other's memories:** two regulars who chat keep how often they've met and the last thing said; a short memory of each other (like the owner's) and things that stick between them would make their friendships richer.
  - **The numbers:** tune pull, fading and the keen week from real nights against the simulation (the night log keeps every regular's pull).
  - **The owner's closeness:** what regulars earn with the owner is the given name; there could be more (the owner saving them the last of the odeng, teasing them).
- **Follow-ups from the Customers talk to each other stage:**
  - **Nobody leaves without a word:** on a busy night, a customer greeted in a rush can run out of time before the owner comes back, and leave after just one line (their goodbye). Move quick-greeted customers up the queue for their first proper word with the owner.
  - **Tune the order prompt:** "order rules" is the most common rejected reply (5–12 a night). The night logs' `rejection_reasons` show which rule; tune the prompt from them and record it in [LLM-TUNING.md](LLM-TUNING.md).
  - **Invented facts (from reading transcripts):** the AI sometimes invents things that are the game's to decide: food that isn't on the menu ("soup's almost done", "I'll ask my wife to make some doenjang jjigae") and prices or a bill ("comes out to be 35,000 won", "two thousand eight hundred won"). (Knowing what nobody's said, and made-up Korean, are fixed: see [LLM-TUNING.md](LLM-TUNING.md).) Find a fix that actually works (not just more rules).
  - **Customer chat share:** tuned (15 Nov): about 18% of lines, mostly in the busy hours. Keep an eye on it with the "Chat chances" and "Owner free" stats as other changes land.
  - **Promises the game doesn't keep:** the owner gives things away the game knows nothing about ("last one free, kid", "it's on me", a customer assuming "the tteokbokki's on the house"), and customers ask for more in ordinary lines ("one more soju, please", "one more beer, I promise it's the last") without a later order happening. Either the game should notice and act (a real later order, a real freebie off the bill), or the AI shouldn't promise what the game won't do.
  - **Order mismatches:** quantities ("just a soju" served two) and "the usual" from first-timers. (Fixed 15 Nov: the wrong drink, "one more soju" served somaek, and lines mentioning rice they didn't order, which now gets the rice.)
  - **Reading pace:** with reading pauses off and the AI answering in under a second, lines can fly by, and a long speech bubble can be replaced before it's read. Waiting for every bubble to be read stopped customers chatting (one voice for the whole tent), so if it's too fast, try a short minimum gap between lines (about a second) rather than a full read, or a reading-speed setting.
- **You turn up as a customer:** a way into the game stage: *you* walk in and take a stool, as one of the night's customers (the old **Next customer** test button, now gone, was the first hint of this).
- **Resume mid-night:** today, a night you quit (closing the window) before 3am isn't counted (Close the bar closes up early instead, and counts), and opening the bar again starts that night afresh at 6pm. Later, the clock, weather and night's takings could be saved, so it carries on where you left.
- **Resume mid-conversation:** if you quit partway through a visit, loading the bar puts the customer back on their stool and the conversation picks up from the saved transcript (if the AI was halfway through a line, that one line is asked for again). Until then, an unfinished visit ends when you quit.
- **Calendar, holidays and events:** the calendar and seasons are in (15 Nov: `rules/seasons.gd`, a day a night through the year), so occasions can hang off the date: Korean occasions (Seollal, Chuseok, Christmas, 11/11, when everyone gives chocolate stick snacks (known by a brand's name, so in the game it's just "11/11"), payday Fridays), **the moon** (its phase worked out from the date, about 29.5 days a cycle, drawn in the sky on clear nights with a few stars; the full moon of the eighth lunar month is Chuseok's, the harvest moon, and Seollal is the second new moon after the winter solstice, so both holidays fall out of the same sum, near enough), and one-off events (a big football match on the overpass screens, a typhoon closing the tent early). Game facts that shape who turns up, what they order and what they talk about.
- **Drinking too much (with a content setting first):** past three or four bottles, someone dozes face-down on the counter (the owner drapes something over them), someone heartbroken gets a situation to make the 2am call to an ex (술김에?! on TV), and once customers come in together, the piggyback home. Alongside **Content boundaries**, since it's drunkenness played for drama.
- **Cliffhangers the night can answer, more often:** the first two nights' recaps both ended on a question nothing in a night can answer ("other"). Watch a few more nights in the stats' HIGHLIGHTS and THE STORY; if it stays that way, steer the recap prompt toward the kinds on the list.
- **Cliffhangers about what's said (a stage of its own):** the cheap kinds are in (16 Nov: the ones the night's events answer). Next, the teasers about what someone says or does: "will she finally confess?", "will he quit?", "is the secret out?". Those need the AI to judge, after a conversation, whether it was answered (like the TV reaction check), with a check and tests for each kind.
- **Drama incidents and story arcs:** K-drama beats across nights: a confession at the counter, a drunk customer the owner sends home in a taxi, a breakup at the next table, the owner's secret slowly coming out, and the classic: redevelopers threatening to tear the tent down. A light **drama director** in the game picks a beat for the night (a game fact), and the AI writes it.
- **Journal and trope book:** a diary of past nights to reread, and, fitting the name *Korean Tent Bar Tropes*, a **trope book** that fills in as tropes appear ("Chaebol heir in disguise ✓", "Rain-soaked confession ✓"). The game tags tropes as it plans each visit, so collecting them is reliable rather than guessed from the text.
- **Regulars book (after the Regulars and relationships stage):** a page per bar, from the title screen and a button in the bar, with a card per regular: their look drawn small, name in Hangul and English, their role (founding regulars), how close they are as a row of soju cups 🍶 filling up from stranger to old friend, how often they come in words ("comes most Fridays", "hasn't been in for a while"), visits, first and last visit, their usual, the things that stuck, the latest summary from each side, and the other regulars they know. A natural first piece of the journal.
- **Variety controls:** partly done: nobody at the counter shares a name, the AI is told tonight's names, jobs and recent appearances to avoid, and the game now decides gender, age range and look. Still to do: variety across nights (archetypes, reasons for coming, names that keep coming back), which matters once regulars exist. Log any repetition in [LLM-TUNING.md](LLM-TUNING.md).
- **More cat:** (people talking to it, and a greedy cat stealing a fish cake, are in: 15 Nov.) customers ordering something for it, a cat that's out all night on a curious streak, or one that only likes certain people. Game events, like the rest of its behaviour.
- **Where people are from:** a home town as a game fact (Seoul, Busan, Daegu, Jeju, a village in Jeolla…), shaping how they talk: a Busan customer with the blunt, warm Gyeongsang satoori (dialect) K-dramas love, written as a flavour in fluent English (the odd "mwo-kko?"-style word, a gruffer rhythm) rather than broken English. Ties into the things that stick ("from Busan").
- **Customers coming in together (after the refactor: it goes deep into arrivals and greeting):** people in the street already walk in twos and threes, a pair sometimes under one umbrella; customers should too. With them, **the bill fight** (계산 전쟁): two people who came together both insisting on paying, decided by kawi-bawi-bo (가위바위보!) on TV, the loser paying for both (a game fact).
  - **Who:** the game decides a group as a fact, now and then: a couple (sharing an umbrella in the rain, the K-drama shot), two colleagues escaping a hoesik, old school friends, a sunbae taking their junior out, three nurses off a night shift. How they know each other is decided, so their first lines can be about it.
  - **Arriving:** they duck under the tarp together, take neighbouring stools (and wait for two free ones, or squeeze in), and are greeted together; each orders, or one orders for both ("two somaek and the pajeon to share").
  - **At the counter:** they already know each other's names, so they talk more than strangers (the customer-chat code with "close" pairs), share dishes (the sharing moments are made for them), and others can still chip in.
  - **Paying:** one pays for both, they fight over the bill (the classic tussle at the till), or they split it, decided by the game.
  - **The story:** a group as a fact in the diary and captions (커플 등장! A COUPLE?!, 회식 탈출! HOESIK ESCAPEES), and regulars who come in together.
- **Speaking for the right person:** in a three- or four-way chat, small models are more likely to write a line for someone else, or a whole exchange. The checks that strip "Name:" and cut off second lines should know every name in the chat.
- **Following one customer:** a way to show just one customer's conversations in the transcript, now that several are mixed together.
- **Long-term relationships:** beyond the Regulars and relationships stage: arcs that play out over many nights between the owner and regulars (and between regulars), such as a regular moving away, a romance at the counter, or an old friend falling out with the owner.
- **Economy:** money, menu upgrades, reputation.
- **Player controls:** set the night's length (time runs faster when the tent's quiet, so it varies), the menu, the weather or day.
- **Weather that changes:** today each night picks one weather at 6pm; it could change during the night (rain setting in after midnight).
- **A better memorable line:** the night summary quotes the longest thing a customer said. The AI could pick the line instead, or the game could favour lines with drama.

### Presentation

- **Character art:** the game decides what someone looks like, from facts it owns; the art system draws it, and the AI never draws anyone directly. Since the People with looks stage, the game picks each person's look first (age range, hair, clothes, colours, extras) and the AI writes to fit it, so every character is drawable and can look the same on every visit, which matters for regulars (save each regular's look with them). Chosen approach, in two steps:
  - **Simple drawn people (done, the People with looks stage):** the game decides age range, hair, clothes, colours, glasses and bags, the AI writes someone who'd look like that, and the game draws them in simple shapes, facing us or from behind. Still to do at this level: different heights and builds, more props that say who someone is (a taxi driver's cap, a school uniform, a phone in hand), and moods showing (slumped shoulders, a hand on the head).
  - **Modular portraits (main approach):** characters assembled from layered parts (face shape, skin tone, hair, eyes, clothes, accessories) like a character creator, with the game mapping each profile to parts (a 58-year-old ajusshi gets greying hair and a cardigan; a 24-year-old office worker gets a blazer). The parts library could come from a free pack (e.g. the CC-BY-SA/GPL "Liberated Pixel Cup" sets, licence-compatible with GPL but heavy on credits, or CC0 packs; most are pixel-art or cartoony rather than K-drama), from a paid or commissioned pack (check the licence suits a GPL project), or from curated AI art (below).
  - **Pre-generated AI art library (a way to fill the parts):** on the desktop, a local image model generates a few hundred portraits or parts in one fixed style, tagged by age, gender, job and so on. A human curates them, and the game ships the keepers and picks the best match. Nothing runs during play, so there's no GPU contention with the LLM and it works on the laptop. Needs effort to keep the style consistent, and a check of the image model's licence.
  - **Live AI portraits (theoretically possible):** a local image model draws each new customer from their profile as they arrive, so everyone is unique. Not planned for now: it competes with the LLM for GPU memory (they'd have to take turns), takes several seconds per image, drifts off-style, can't run on the laptop, and sometimes produces something unusable. Worth revisiting if local image models get faster and more consistent, or if we ever have GPU memory to spare.
- **Ambience (sound and animation):** the tent should feel alive even when nobody's talking. Driven by facts the game owns (weather, time of night, what's cooking, how busy it is), so it always matches what's happening, and it needs no AI. The first version is built (the Atmosphere and Sound and music stages), all made in code; what's left:
  - **Ambient sound:** done with placeholders made in code (the Sound and music stage). Next, swap in real recordings one at a time, starting with the ones that sound most synthetic (the murmur, the pour, the cat, the cash tin), then rain, traffic and the rest. CC0 sources: Freesound (filter by licence) and Kenney's audio packs; log everything in `CREDITS.md`. Each sound is one entry in `ambience.gd`, so a recording can replace it without changing the rest.
  - **Music:** Arirang, arranged in code, is the placeholder. Later: a real recording or arrangement (a public-domain tune performed for the project, or a CC0 track), perhaps more tunes (other folk songs, or a lo-fi theme), and music from the old radio in bars that have one.
    - **Varying the music:** more public-domain tunes of the kind Korean TV and K-dramas use, arranged in code like Arirang: other Korean folk songs (Doraji taryeong, Milyang and Jindo Arirang, Ganggang-sullae, Saeya saeya), and the classical pieces variety shows love for comic and dramatic moments (Flight of the Bumblebee for a rush, a Chopin nocturne at 2am, Vivaldi, the Can-can). Chosen by the game from the scene (the rush, the small hours, a big moment, the diary), never playing over a reaction's sting. The music is the composer's (long out of copyright); the arrangement is ours.
  - **Background animation:** swinging bulbs, rain, snow, fog, drips off the tarp, passers-by with umbrellas, steam off the odeng pot and the sky changing through the night are done. Since 15 Nov: steam off hot dishes on the counter, and a flickering 노래방 neon sign. Also: snow and rain landing on umbrellas, customers swaying after a couple of bottles, a city behind the tent whose windows go dark through the night (APT blocks, office towers, neon crosses), and dishes drawn from what they come in (each menu item's `art`, so bar-specific menus get pictures for free). Still to do: the moon and stars (see Calendar), more vessels and bits as dishes are added, and the occasional street cat. (Cars on the overpass were tried and taken out: lights above the tent looked wrong.) Godot handles these cheaply with particles, tweens and simple animations, so they run fine on the laptop.
  - **Weather changing during the night:** the weather is already something you see and hear; when it can change during the night (see **Weather that changes**), the sound and visuals should fade across smoothly.
  - **Hangul on the sign:** the sign uses the system's font fallback for Hangul. If it shows boxes on any machine, bundle a free Korean font (e.g. Noto Sans KR, under the OFL) and log it in `LICENSE-ASSETS.md`.
  - **Passers-by together:** done (15 Nov): twos and threes, phones, umbrellas of different shapes and sizes, and people running for it. Next: customers who come in together (see **Groups**).
- **Voices:** characters "speak" their lines, with a setting to choose **off**, **babble**, **system voices** or **AI voices**. The game picks each character's voice from facts it owns (gender, age, mood), never the AI.
  - **Babble:** Animal Crossing-style babble: a short syllable sound per letter or word, pitched and paced per character (deeper and slower for an old ajusshi, quicker when nervous). No AI, works on any machine, and suits the cosy tone. Built in Godot from a few CC0 syllable sounds, pitch-shifted. The first one to build.
  - **System voices:** Godot's built-in text-to-speech, which reads lines aloud with the voices installed in Windows. No setup and no extra software, and it works on the laptop. The voices sound robotic and the choice depends on what Windows has installed, so it's mainly a quick way to test whether spoken lines add anything, and a fallback when no AI voice service is running.
  - **AI voices:** a local text-to-speech model reads the lines aloud, running as a separate local service like Ollama, with Godot playing the audio. Candidates: **Kokoro** (small 82M model, Apache-2.0 licence, good quality, runs on CPU) and **Piper** (fast on CPU, GPL-3.0 engine, many downloadable voices, each with its own licence to check). Run it on the CPU so it doesn't fight the LLM for GPU memory. Things to check first: how they pronounce Korean names and words, and whether any voices suit the setting.
- **Speech bubbles, next steps:** bubbles are in (15 Nov). Later: a taller stage (or a strip above the tent) if bubbles cover the sign too much, text size with the other settings, and remembering the Bubbles checkbox between runs.
- **Korean terms with English hover text, next steps:** done for the transcript, narration, the info panel and the diary (15 Nov: `rules/glossary.gd`). Still to do: more words as they turn up in transcripts (the stats count which are said), and perhaps a glossary page.
- **AI-generated food art (much later):** a local image model on the 3080 could draw dishes, including signature ones. It would compete with the LLM for GPU memory and struggles to keep a consistent style, so it's a much-later idea rather than the plan.
- **3D graphics option:** a 3D version of the tent (e.g. low-poly CC0 models from Quaternius or Kenney) as an alternative to the 2D view. Keep the simulation logic separate from the visuals so either view can sit on top of it. Mind GPU memory: the local LLM shares the graphics card.

### Lower priority

- **Other languages:** a Korean-language mode (and maybe others), since the LLM can write them. Voices, the Korean-terms glossary and the gendered-term checks would all need work.
- **Player customisation:** the style guide, archetypes and menu catalogue as editable files, so players can make their own version of the bar.
- **Designing the game stage:** decide what the player actually does when they join the conversation (pick from dialogue choices, or type freely to the AI customers) and what changes as a result (mood, tips, whether they come back, story beats). Worth designing before that stage starts.
- **Non-Korean visitors:** tourists and expats, who would naturally use Western name order (see the name-order note in [LLM-TUNING.md](LLM-TUNING.md)).
- **Non-binary owners:** owners are a woman or a man for now, because their title (Ajumma or Ajusshi) depends on it; a non-binary owner would need a title that works (perhaps just Sajangnim, boss).
