# KTBT: Korean Tent Bar Tropes

*A pojangmacha simulation with AI-generated K-drama characters.*

## The idea

A cosy, Coffee Talk-style scene set in a pojangmacha under a Seoul overpass. Over the course of a night, AI-invented customers turn up, order soju and street food, and chat with the tent's owner. Each customer is generated to fit the time of night, so the after-work crowd gives way to night-shift workers and heartbroken stragglers as the hours pass.

**A night** runs from 6pm to 3am on a clock that always runs: at most about five minutes of real time, since time runs faster while the tent's quiet (6× when it's empty, 2× with one customer, normal from two). About twenty customers arrive, at times that suit the hour: a trickle after work, a rush from 9pm to 1am, and nobody new after 2:30am. At 3am the owner calls last orders; once the last customer has gone, a summary shows the night's customers and most memorable line, and the bar is ready for another night. Each bar goes round the week, night by night.

**The tent** has five stools and one owner, who is also the only cook. Drinks and banchan come straight away; cooked dishes queue at the stove, the AI is told what's still cooking, and the owner carries each dish over as soon as it's ready. The owner talks to one customer at a time and moves round: greeting newcomers first (quickly when it's busy), then settling up with whoever's leaving, otherwise chatting with whoever's been left alone longest. Where they stand is about their hands first: they go over to a stool to pour, set something down, carry a dish, settle up, or for a moment worth it (tears, a kindness), and come back; while something's cooking they stay at the stove, stirring, and talk to whoever it is from there (the AI's told, so it can work it in); with nothing to cook or carry, they go over to whoever they're talking to. Now and then, customers chat to each other instead, and sometimes the owner or another customer joins in. When every stool is taken, newcomers wait a while, then walk on. Now and then two people come in together (the game decides what they are to each other: a couple, colleagues escaping a hoesik, old school friends, a sunbae taking their hoobae out, or two close regulars): they wait for two stools side by side, know each other's names, talk to each other first, leave together, and settle the bill the way the game decides (one pays for both, they fight over it and kawi-bawi-bo settles it, or they split it).

**The player is the show's PD.** The night is an episode of a show, and it runs itself: orders are served, people talk, the owner works. The player is its PD (producer-director), and nothing they do pauses it: they choose whose story the camera follows (that person gets the airtime; following the owner, the owner does: customers chat among themselves less, and the owner lets a little more of themselves show), and play a few **plant cards** a night (a regular rung and asked to come down, a sudden downpour, a phone ringing at the counter, a chaebol's driver come to fetch someone, a word from the show's sponsor), things that happen, which the people they happen to take up or don't. When a moment gets a TV caption, the PD has five seconds to pick its spin: the AI's own, the opposite, or a cheeky roast in the editors' voice. Two cards a night are **write-ins**: the PD types it (a cue card held up behind the camera, an interview question, a text someone gets, some gossip, a breaking-news headline, a pop quiz), and the AI answers the player's own words, framed as what happened in the scene, never as instructions, and checked like any line. Doing nothing is always fine. Everyone at the counter knows they're on a show. Typing to the customers directly comes later.

**Let the crew run it.** From the menu, the PD can hand the show to the crew for so many nights: the desk and opening the next night play themselves (as autoplay does for testing), and the PD watches, everything they'd only look at still working. A badge on the top bar says the crew are running it, what would upset the run waits, and Stop asks whether to finish tonight first or take over there and then.

### The PD's pins

Any line said can be pinned for the episode with its 📌 in the transcript: three a night, as "Previously on…" is three things, so a fourth drops the oldest. The pins are the night's quote, they go into the owner's diary's facts, and the next night's "Previously on…" makes its beats out of them, quoting each under its beat. Nothing pinned, and the editors choose, as before.

### The edit suite

At 3am you're closing up, so the edit's the next day: as the bar opens (while it loads, while "Previously on…" plays, or early in the night, until the first customer) you can step in as the PD, and nothing ever waits for you to. The suite has the writers' cut picked: you can change the episode's title (three the AI drafted overnight, mixing Korean and English as TV does, or your own), the scenes "Previously on…" is made of (up to three, your pins ticked to start with, from the big moments, what happened, the customers' posts and the viewers' comments; the recap's rewritten from them, while TV pop-ups about the edit keep the screen going) and its cliffhanger (two or three the night can answer, which bring their people in now and then, as TV does; or your own, which can't). "Previously on…" then plays your cut, a post or comment you ticked slammed on beside its beat as its card. Never stepping in, the writers' goes out: now and then (when anything went online, and you didn't pin three lines) it ends on a post or comment, as K-TV cuts to 네티즌 반응 (netizens react): "The internet had opinions…"

### The ratings (시청률)

The ratings are the game's score: customers pay their bills as at any bar, but the bar's takings aren't kept or shown. Every episode gets its score at 3am, worked out as Korean TV's ratings are read: on TV, the share of Korea's TV homes said to be watching (Nielsen Korea measures the real ones); lower down the ladder (below), the same score shown as views or subscribers. On TV ours is a late-night show, so a few percent is an ordinary night, 5 to 8% a good one, double digits (두 자릿수!) a hit, and 20% the stuff of legend. The game works it out from what happened, no AI: the big moments that got a reaction (each a little less than the last), a mix of moods as they aired (so the PD's spin counts; a laugh and a tear beat three tears), last time's cliffhanger coming true, regulars back, new regulars and people on closer terms, the PD's cards that were taken up, a full house, a drinking game, the cat's theft, the first snow, the counter cheering, and a little luck. It's on the diary page by the owner's name, with a hover saying what ratings are and where tonight's came from; a hit, 20% or a new best for the show (자체 최고 시청률!) gets a banner. "Previously on…" shows last time's. The last three nights' ratings nudge how often customers come by, ±10% at most: it's still mostly about the cosy.

The ratings are on screen all night (시청률 with the number): before a night, last time's; during it, tonight's so far, worked out the same way as things happen but without the luck (that's only known at 3am); then tonight's. Their hover's a short summary (where the show goes out, what it takes to move on, how the season's going); a click opens the side panel's The show, with the whole story: who films it (the niece or nephew, by name and handle), where it goes out, what the number means, the rule and the progress, tonight's number and where it came from, the last episodes and the channels it's been on. The side panel's Stats has a RATINGS section with the parts, the last nights' ratings, and how much busier they make tonight.

### Seasons

Episodes come in seasons of eight, and are only ever numbered within their season, as Korean TV does (시즌 2 · 제3화: season 2, episode 3, "S2 · EP 3"), never as a running count: on the diary page, "Previously on…", the edit suite, and in what the AI's told. (A night from before seasons is a pilot, 파일럿.) The eighth is the finale (on the top bar, "S1 · EP 8/8 · FINALE", and said as the tarp goes up). At 3am after it, once the diary's written, the season's looked back on in the diary's place (시즌 결산): an episode a row, with its title, a bar for its ratings and the line pinned that night (the best episode in yellow), and at the bottom the channel's verdict, stamped on. The verdict is the game's, no AI: the season's average ratings, plus a bonus for a new best for the show or a hit some night, against the targets of the rung the show's on (below): a season good enough moves the show up a rung (대박 시즌!); a quiet one is a warning (위기!), and two in a row on the same rung drop it one (강등!); anything else is renewed (재계약!; on the phone stream there's nothing to renew, so the stream just goes on, 계속!). Each stamp explains itself on hover, with how the verdict was worked out and the rung's targets. The verdict's kept with the bar, and the next night is the new season's first, on its new rung if it moved ("Your show's been picked up by an online video channel!"), or renewed by its channel, by name ("Moonlight TV has renewed the show for season 3."). The loading screen's channel bug says which channel the show's on. The title screen says where each bar's show has got to ("season 2 on an online video channel, 3 of 8 aired"), and "Previously on…"'s episode card says where tonight's episode is.

### The platform ladder

The show climbs or drops a ladder of three platforms, season by season, and is never taken off air: a phone stream (a new bar's first episodes, posted on a short-video app), an online video channel, and network TV, the top (where every bar from before the ladder is). No real platform, channel or show is ever named. The night's score is worked out the same on every rung (the ratings above); each rung shows it in its own units and words: views and likes on the phone stream ("11,400 views · 680 likes", 조회수 11.4K in the top bar), the online video channel's total subscribers, won and lost episode by episode (each episode brings some and loses about 300 to unsubscribes, so a thin one can lose more than it wins: "12,400 subscribers (+390) · 86,000 views", 구독자 subscribers 12.4K; kept with the show, so it's still there if the show moves on and comes back), and 시청률 ratings on TV; a hit off TV has blown up (떡상!), and a new best is the show's most views (자체 최고 조회수!). Each episode's numbers are kept with the rung it aired on, so the diary and the season page always show them as they were. Moving on is to absolute targets in each rung's own units, as platforms really work (and never the score: 10 is a great rating, and a handful of views): a season averaging 1,000+ views a night gets the stream its own online video channel; 10,000 subscribers by the end of a season gets the channel picked up by network TV, and ending two seasons under 1,000 drops it back to the stream; on TV, a season averaging 10% is a hit, and two seasons averaging under 5% drop the show back to the channel. Ending two seasons in a row under 1,000 subscribers drops the channel back to the phone stream, the bottom rung. It's paced so a great season moves the show up in one and a good one in two: a good night's about 1,100 views on the stream (a good season just makes it; a so-so one doesn't), and on the channel a good episode nets about +1,050 subscribers (8,400 a season: two to reach 10,000) and a great one about +1,500 (12,000: one). The rule for moving up or down, and how this season's going against it ("This season so far: 640 views a night (1,000 to move on)", "8,240 of 10,000 subscribers"), are on the hover of the numbers in the top bar (and the rule on the diary page's). Rungs change the numbers, never the PD's tools: the player can do everything from the first night. The show's filmed by the owner's niece or nephew (the game decides which; the AI names them, in Hangul too, and their stream's handle): on the phone stream it's their own stream (@jiwoo_pocha_live), and when the camera turns on someone, the prompts say it's them, on their phone. Every other channel the show's booked on is named by the AI (in Hangul and English, with what it means, on hover: 달빛TV, Moonlight TV), checked so it's never a real broadcaster, platform or app, nor one the show's been on before; offline, the game's own. A move announces it ("Your show's been picked up by network TV, 달빛TV (Moonlight TV)!"), and the bar keeps every channel it's been on. The top bar says it in Korean and English ("조회수 views 11.4K so far · phone stream"), with the hover saying more.

While a new bar's being made, the loading screen offers "📋 The PD sets up the show" (or Enter): a setup screen, the game's picks ticked, where for now the PD chooses where the show starts on the ladder (each rung described: its units and what it takes to move up or down). Starting higher skips the climb, which is mostly for trying the rungs out. The bar waits while it's open; not stepping in, the show starts as a phone stream. More of the show's setup will go here.

### The PD's cards

- **A pack of tropey cards, six dealt a night** from their own dice (so dealing never shifts anything else's rolls): three events, two **write-ins** and a word from our sponsor (PPL, with the show's own made-up sponsors). Not last night's cards again, and not one that can't happen tonight (no first snow in summer); one that can't happen *yet* stays in the hand, greyed out with why.
- **Two kinds:** a **situation** (something that happens, told to whoever it happens to, as what just happened and as a moment for their next line) is a data entry and the words that show it was taken up, no code; a **fact** (it changes something the game owns: the weather, an arrival, a round on someone's bill, the lights) is a data entry and a small handler. A write-in is a situation with a blank the PD fills. Many cards have variants (who's calling, how the two know each other, which song), picked as they're dealt.
- **Big moments linger:** a card that happens to someone is the moment for their next line, and the bigger ones (the ex walking past, a text that changes everything, a phone call) stay on their minds for a few lines after (`lingers`), in their talk with the owner or a chat they start or join: the talk can come back to it, but needn't every line. The cue card keeps its subject the topic in the same way. The tent's own cards (the rain, the song, the power going, the first snow) are everyone's: each customer's next line, the owner's next line too, and on everyone's minds for a couple of lines after; and a top-up order due just then waits a turn.
- **Aimed with the camera:** a card that happens to someone happens to whoever the camera's on (with nobody followed, the game picks someone, and the card's hover says who). The tent's cards (the rain, the song) are for everyone.
- **Write-ins, the PD's own words:** a one-line box on the desk, never a pop-up, while the night carries on. What's typed is framed as what happened in the scene, never as instructions, and every reply still goes through every check, so typed words can't change a game fact. What's typed is checked too (length, hard limits, no teasing about looks), and any Korean in it is explained for the AI. Offline, write-ins are greyed out (the canned lines can't answer free text).
- **Counted:** each card played, whether the lines given it took it up, and whether it came up in anyone's lines while it mattered (for a card aimed at someone, as long as they're here).

## Core design rule

**The game owns the facts; the AI owns the flavour.**

- The game decides the time, weather, menu, prices, seats, when people arrive and leave, and how everyone looks (gender, age range, hair, clothes).
- The AI invents people and dialogue *within* those limits.
- Anything the AI returns that the game depends on (customer profiles, orders) comes back as JSON and is checked by the game. An order for something not on the menu is rejected and retried.

## Characters

### The owner
- Invented by the AI **once per bar (save)** and then kept. They're the constant heart of the tent.
- **How they look:** the game decides (their age range, hair, clothes and colours, always with an apron), and the AI invents someone who'd look like that.
- **Their diary:** every night is an episode, and at 3am the owner writes it up in their diary, in their own voice, from the facts the game picked (the big moments, who came, the regulars). First, a TV-style end card: the night's biggest moment again, its caption and face big over the whole window under 오늘의 명장면 (tonight's best moment); then the page, with a polaroid or two stuck down its edge (whoever had that moment, then a regular or whoever stayed longest, each in their big moment's face or calm, a handwritten name under it). The diaries pile up into the bar's story, a book you can flip back through from the bar or the title screen. Opening a bar plays "Previously on…": a K-drama recap of last time, done over the top in a different K-TV style each time (melodrama, makjang, variety show, nostalgic).
- **Their life is fixed:** a secret they only hint at, their family ("widowed young; a grown daughter in Daejeon") and what they did before the tent. The AI writes these once, the game keeps them, and every conversation has to fit them, so the owner's story never changes.
- Every customer talks to the owner, and sometimes customers talk to each other too.
- The player makes the show (see above); typing to the customers comes later.

### Customers
- Generated per visit, shaped by the time of night, the day and the weather, in two steps: first a name and gender (so a name that's already at the counter can be swapped quickly), then the rest.
- Profile (JSON): name, gender, age, job, mood, why they're here tonight, how they talk, and their look.
- **How they look:** decided by the game, like gender: an age range, hair (by gender and age), clothes (by the weather), colours (mostly beige and black, as in Seoul), and sometimes glasses or a bag. The AI invents someone who'd look like that, and the game draws them: facing us as they come and go, and from behind once they're sat at the counter.
- **Names in Hangul:** every name (customers, the owner, the cat) is shown in Hangul too, before the romanised one, like the sign, so the player gets used to seeing Korean names. The AI writes it; the game checks it fits and leaves it off rather than show it wrong.
- **Gender:** decided by the game: half women, half men, and about one in a hundred non-binary (they/them). The AI names them. Shown on screen as ♀ ♂ ⚲ next to names, since Korean names don't make it obvious.
- **Game facts about each customer:** how long they'll stay (30–60 game minutes, longer for someone here to talk), their mood for company (chatty, keeps to themselves, or here to talk to the owner: someone keeping to themselves never starts a chat, but a chatty one with nobody else to talk to may turn to them, once a night), whether they're hungry or having a rough night, and whether they'll order more later.
- Visits: arrive, order from the menu, eat and drink, talk with the owner (and maybe other customers), then say goodbye, pay and leave when their time's up.
- **Nobody knows more than they should:** the AI is told what everyone can see (how someone looks) and that nobody knows another's name, age, job, reason for coming or secret until it's been said. Everyone speaks fluent, natural English (they're speaking Korean), with the odd Korean word a K-drama fan would know.
- **What conversations turn to:** the game picks a topic for each spell of chat (their day, what's on their mind, a story from the owner's past, the weather, the cat, food, the owner's nagging, a silly food fight, an embarrassing story, teasing between friends…), so talk doesn't get stuck on one thing. The night has a shape: lighter before midnight, more open after it.
- **Strangers talk politely:** nobody at the counter knows anyone yet, so terms like oppa, hyung, nuna and eonni (for people who are close) are ruled out, and no two people there share a name.
- **Regulars:** every bar opens with two long-standing regulars, each with a role in the tent; new customers can become regulars, who come back more the more regular they are, fade away if they stop coming, and come back as themselves (the same name, look and personality, their usual). Everyone knows only what they've actually learned: whose names have been said, how many visits, what each knows about the other. Every pair that matters (the owner and a regular, two regulars) has one bond: how they know each other, how often they've met, whose name each knows, how warm it's got and how close they are. After each visit, the owner and the customer each remember the gist (a sentence or two) in their bond, older memories blurring into an impression; the things that stick (job, family, home town, a pet, a worry, a hope) are kept if they were really said, and known only by whoever heard them (the owner, or the people in that chat); a line that contradicts what someone's said about their family (a widower's "my wife's nagging") is sent back; something coming up that they mention (an interview on Monday) is on the owner's mind next time they come in, and how it went is remembered. At closing, regulars who chatted remember each other (a memory each, a fact, anything coming up), and every line is told who the speaker knows at the counter, from their own side only. Closeness pays off: close regulars talk casually, take the stool next to each other, wave each other over at the door and drift in around the same time, and the owner keeps the last of the odeng back for a close regular now and then. The AI's verdict on the night decides whether someone comes back. Who may call whom what (oppa, eonni, imo, sajangnim…) is a game rule, from gender, age, owner or customer and closeness; regulars who chat get to know each other, and after a few meetings the younger may ask to use a friendlier term, which is allowed from then on. Terms are coloured by warmth on screen, with their meaning on hover.

### The cat
- Every bar has a cat, saved with it: the game picks its coat, personality (lazy, affectionate, greedy, grumpy or curious) and sex (she or he), and it's a stray that wandered in and stayed, so it isn't anyone's: the owner and the regulars gave it a nickname, the kind Koreans give street cats (나비 Nabi, butterfly; 고등어 Godeungeo, mackerel, for a striped tabby; 치즈 Chijeu, cheese, for a ginger), which the AI picks from the game's list for its coat (no two bars' cats share one, and never a person's name). Its meaning is on its hover, beside its face (a portrait for each coat), and its face is slapped onto the TV captions about it, as people's are: going out on patrol, clocking back in, claiming a lap (beside whoever's), stealing a skewer (beside the victim).
- A greedy cat sometimes steals a fish cake skewer off someone's table and bolts (a game fact, with the red light). Eyeing someone's food, it may instead be slipped a bit from their plate (likelier the greedier it is). Asleep on someone's lap when their time's up, it keeps them a little longer (they can't get up, and say so). Someone says its name, and now and then it answers (야옹, ya-ong), but mostly it couldn't care less.
- The game decides where it is all night (asleep by the stove, on someone's lap, out in the street…), by its personality, and everyone talks about it as it really is: nobody strokes it unless it's on their lap.

### The tent's look
- **Every bar looks like its own:** the AI chooses its look to suit the owner and the name, from options the game offers, and it's saved with the bar. The sign shows the name in Hangul with the romanised name under it, in wood, neon, banner or lightbox style. The tent cover has a shape (classic, peaked, scalloped, awning or rounded) and a colour (ten, three of them striped); each new bar is offered a random shortlist of colours and shapes, so they vary. One or two little touches finish it off (string lights, lanterns, a lucky cat…).
- **Everyone's own face:** the owner and the regulars get faces made for them by the image model on this PC, in the gaps when the GPU's free (as a new bar's made, and after a night closes), and then the same face in each TV mood for the captions; until a mood's made (or if it came out looking like someone else), their calm face stands in on the TV. Everyone else gets the nearest of the library's faces, repainted in their colours.
- **Speech bubbles:** each line also pops up over whoever's speaking, with who it's to and a line to the listener; the transcript keeps everything.
- **Korean TV captions:** the night is edited like a variety show. When something noteworthy happens, a caption pops up in Hangul and English with a sound sting ("짠! JJAN! CHEERS!", "폭풍 흡입! INHALING IT!", and for emergencies a flashing red light and siren). The game decides when and owns the words, and for the big moments in a conversation the AI writes the caption while the game keeps the Korean ("충격! SHE QUIT HER JOB?!").
- **What you hear:** the street, the weather and the tent (rain on the tarp, traffic on the overpass, the odeng pot, sizzling while something cooks, the counter's murmur, glasses and stools), all following what the game knows is happening, and Arirang now and then.
- **The night around it:** the sky darkens through the night and the tent's glow changes with it; behind the tent, the city (APT blocks with their numbers on the side, office towers, neon crosses), each bar's own, its windows going dark as the night goes on; the nights run through the year's seasons (with the summer monsoon), and the weather follows on from night to night, with the first snow of each winter a moment of its own; the weather is something you see (rain, snow, fog, haze, a swinging bulb in a breeze); and most people in the street walk past without coming in, busiest in the evening: alone, in twos and threes, on their phones, under umbrellas of all shapes when it's wet (customers fold theirs down under the tarp), or now and then running for it.
- **Korean words explained:** the menu, the words people say at a pocha (banchan, jjan, aigoo, hwaiting…) and the terms of address are coloured, and explain themselves on hover. The rule: any Korean that stays on screen (the bar's sign, the neon across the street) has a hover saying what it is, and so does any Hangul in the transcript, the info panel and the diary (captions, names, stamps), so you can tell a translation from something else; captions and recaps that pop up and go show their English alongside instead. The game's own Hangul has its meaning written once and checked by a test; Korean the AI invents (a bar's name, later a dish's) comes with its meaning in the same reply, kept as a game fact; the AI never translates live, since its Korean isn't reliable.
- **Conditions, not scripts:** the game never hands the AI a line to say. Now and then it sets a scene instead, only when it's true: how someone orders tonight, a craving ("rain like this always makes them crave pajeon and makgeolli"), a table custom (nobody pours their own glass), a chance to share. The AI decides whether anything comes of it.

## Saving and loading

Each **save is one bar**: its owner, its regulars, its history. You can have several and come back to any of them.

- **Title screen:** *New bar* or a list of saved bars (bar name, owner, nights and visits and last played), each of which can be opened or deleted (two clicks, so a stray one can't lose a bar).
- **New bar:** the AI invents the owner and the bar's name (a Korean name, checked by the game like everything else the AI writes), and a new save is created straight away.
- **Autosave, always.** The game saves as it goes, with no save button and no "your progress will be lost" prompt. Close the window at any time, and next time you load, it carries on with the next customer.
- **When it saves:** when a bar is created, after every visit, at the end of each night, when you close the bar, and when the window is closed.
- **What's saved:** the bar's name and its meaning, the owner, the cat, the visit and night counts, a short history of recent visits (who came, what they ordered, the bill), a log of recent nights (day, weather, customers, memorable line), the day of the week it started on, and later the menu and (from the Regulars and relationships stage) regulars and what they remember.
- **Closing up early:** **Close the bar** during a night closes up early: a 마감 CLOSED board goes out front and a bulb goes off, nobody new comes in, and the owner tells the counter why (her own reason, from the AI, also on TV). Whoever's here finishes their night at their own pace; once the tent's empty the night ends there, counted, with the owner's diary (giving the same reason) and a 조기 마감 CLOSED EARLY stamp. From the diary page you can open the next night or go back to the title.
- **Quitting mid-night:** closing the window instead (its X, or Alt-F4) just saves and quits: for now, a night you leave that way isn't counted, and opening the bar again starts that night afresh at 6pm; resuming mid-night is a later idea. An unfinished visit simply ends when you quit. Resuming mid-conversation (the customer still on their stool, the chat picking up from the saved transcript) is a later idea in [PLAN.md](PLAN.md).
- **Safe writes:** each save is written to a temporary file and then swapped in, so a crash mid-save can't corrupt the bar.
- **Format:** one JSON file per bar in Godot's `user://saves/` folder (on Windows: `%APPDATA%\Godot\app_userdata\KTBT\saves\`). It's human-readable, which is handy for debugging, or for peeking at what the AI invented.

## K-drama style guide

Included in every AI prompt so all characters feel like they belong to the same show. The AI riffs on these rather than copying them.

**Setting:** a tarp tent (each bar's own shape and colour), plastic stools, green soju bottles, steam off the tteokbokki, bare bulbs swinging, rain more often than not.

**Owner archetypes:**
- The gruff-but-soft ajumma who scolds customers for not eating properly
- The quiet ajusshi with a mysterious past who gives one line of perfect advice
- The ex-something (gangster, chef, idol trainee) who left it all behind

**Customer archetypes:**
- Salarymen escaping a hoesik (company dinner)
- A newly dumped office worker drinking alone
- Exhausted nurses after a night shift
- A chaebol heir in disguise
- Students celebrating the end of exams
- A taxi driver on his break
- Old friends reuniting after years apart
- Someone working up the courage to confess their feelings

**Tone:** warm, melodramatic, a little melancholy. "One more bottle", unspoken feelings, late-night honesty, with humour mixed in.

### Cheeky, not "bants"

The tent isn't all sad: friends rib each other, the owner nags, people argue about whether the sauce goes on or the pork goes in it. That cheek is affection, and the game keeps it that way. What it never does is the kind of "bants" some people use as a cover for harassment, sexual or otherwise.

- **Affection, and both sides know it:** only between people who are already close, or from the owner to a regular. Never at strangers, never at someone who has just sat down.
- **Down or sideways, never a pile-on:** one person teases, the table doesn't gang up.
- **What you do, not who you are:** slurping noodles, losing the table game, the karaoke song, still not texting back. Never bodies, looks, weight, gender, sexuality, age as an insult, or anything sexual. Being single only if they brought it up.
- **They can give it back:** if the target can't answer, it isn't teasing.
- **The owner is the line:** when someone goes too far, the owner shuts it down.

This holds at every content setting. A future Adult setting can have adult things in it, between grown-ups who want them. It still won't have people who use "it's just a joke" to justify hurting someone.

### Real people, brands and shows

The game is full of Korean references, and should be. But what's in it is written live by an AI nobody reviews, so it never uses real people, brands or shows by name. (Not legal advice: just staying well clear. Korean TV is famously careful about this too, blurring logos and inventing brands.) The joke works without the name.

- **No real people:** no celebrities, TV chefs, idols, actors, athletes or politicians, in dialogue, captions, the diary, recaps, or anything else the game shows. Describe the trope instead: "salts it from shoulder height like a TV chef showing off", not the chef's name.
- **No brands or products:** "mix coffee" (믹스커피) or "a vending-machine coffee stick", not the brand; "the green bottle" of soju, not its maker; "the convenience store", not the chain. Generic Korean food and drink names are fine (soju, makgeolli, banana milk, ramyeon, mix coffee). No logos in the art, ever.
- **No real shows, films, songs or games by name:** "that fridge cooking show", "a K-drama", "a variety show", "the game he plays all night".
- **What's fine:** real places (Seoul, Busan, Hongdae, the Han river), public holidays and customs (Chuseok, Seollal), public-domain music (Arirang and other folk songs, classical pieces), and the tropes themselves.
- **Our own stand-ins:** when a reference needs a name, the game can own a fictional one (a celebrity chef, a coffee brand, a drama everyone's watching), clearly our own and not a near-copy of a real name.
- **The same for anything public:** the store page, screenshots, the trailer, the game's own text. (The design docs can mention real games and shows as inspiration, like *Coffee Talk*: that's talking about the game, not what's in it.)
- **How it's kept:** the AI's style guide says so, and lines, captions, diaries and recaps naming an obvious brand are sent back (`Talk.REAL_BRANDS`). Real people can't be listed reliably, so for them it's the prompt; anything that slips through goes in [LLM-TUNING.md](LLM-TUNING.md).

## Menu and ordering

Each bar has its own menu, chosen when it opens from a catalogue of real pocha dishes (`scripts/rules/catalogue.gd`): the staples everywhere (soju, beer, a bowl of rice), most of the pocha classics, the owner's own picks to suit them, and their signature dishes, on a board in the tent you can click to read. The AI chooses within the game's house rules (how many dishes, prices within each dish's range). Bars from before menus, and the tests, keep the **classic menu** (`scripts/rules/menu.gd`):

- **Drinks:** soju, makgeolli, beer, somaek (soju dropped in beer)
- **Mains:** tteokbokki, sundae, dakgalbi, pajeon, ramyeon
- **Sides:** odeng (fish cake skewers), gyeran-mari, gonggibap (a bowl of rice), bokkeumbap (fried rice made in the leftover sauce; only ordered later, after a main)
- **Free banchan** (kimchi and pickled radish) comes with every order and isn't on the bill.
- **Signature dishes:** one or two of the owner's own, invented from their story ("grandma's knife-cut noodles"), first on the board under 대표 메뉴.
- **Tonight's specials:** the game picks a dish from the bar's own menu by the weather and the season (rain is always a pancake and makgeolli), now and then the owner's own dish or a drink worth pushing, never the staples or last night's again; marked on the menu customers see.
- **Sold out:** now and then a dish has run out: struck through on the board, and it can't be ordered.
- **Menus change slowly:** every few weeks, a dish hardly anyone orders comes off the board and the owner chooses something seasonal to go on instead (never a regular's usual, never the classics or their own dishes). The diary mentions it.

**How people order** (game rules, checked on every AI order):

- **What they say is what they get:** an order line that names a different drink or main is asked for again; a side it asks for but the AI left out is added.
- **First order:** at least one drink and one food, because nobody drinks on an empty stomach. Usually one main and one drink, maybe a side. The game decides who's **hungry** (may order two mains) or having a **rough night** (may order two drinks).
- **Later orders:** half of customers order once; the rest order more partway through the conversation (one more bottle, a side, rice), and a few order a third time. Never a new main, at most one more drink each time, and it has to sound like ordering more.
